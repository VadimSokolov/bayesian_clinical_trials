# Chapter 1. Why Bayesian Trials?

This chapter asks why a clinical trial should be designed as a decision and why a researcher would want a Bayesian approach. It opens with Florence Nightingale and the Scutari hospital, then with SELECT, the semaglutide cardiovascular outcomes trial, and a simulation of what a Bayesian design with the same one sided false positive limit of 2.5 percent would have saved in events, trial cost, sales and patients. It sets out the price of a trial in dollars, failure rates and years, reviews evidence on the efficiency of real adaptive and Bayesian trials and on whether the approach is spreading (registry mention rates), and states the counterweight: the saving comes from monitoring, multiplicity and prior information and not from a lower bar, and in REWIND a scheduled design detected the benefit more often. It then recasts a trial as a measurement and a decision, where a prior, a utility and a rule say when acting beats waiting. It previews the thesis and four mechanisms (checking the data often against one bar, analyzing several treatments together, informative priors and optimal stopping), says what a regulator needs, gives a short history and maps the book. The traditional trial itself is set out in the chapter on testing.
